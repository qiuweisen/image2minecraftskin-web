## Quick Answer

**What is position sizing?**
Position sizing is the method of determining the exact number of shares or contracts to trade to control risk.

**How is it calculated?**
Your position size is determined by four factors:
1. **Account Size**: Your total trading capital.
2. **Risk Per Trade**: The percentage of your account you are willing to lose (e.g., 1%).
3. **Entry Price**: Where you enter the trade.
4. **Stop-Loss Distance**: The difference between your entry and stop-loss price.

**The Formula:**
`Position Size = (Account Size × Risk %) ÷ (Entry Price − Stop-Loss Price)`

**Why are 1% or 2% rules often discussed?**
These percentages are common reference points used to help traders avoid large drawdowns during inevitable losing streaks. However, position sizing **does not guarantee profitability**; it is simply a defensive tool to help preserve capital.

## Key Takeaways

- **Position sizing manages risk, not returns**: It defines how much you lose when you are wrong, not how much you make when you are right.
- **Risk percentage is not universal**: The 1% or 2% guidelines are common, but the right risk level depends on your experience, strategy, and market volatility.
- **Recovery math is asymmetric**: A 50% account drawdown requires a 100% gain just to break even.
- **Practice makes permanent**: Use a chart replay simulator to practice calculating position sizes before risking real capital.

## What is Position Sizing?

[Position sizing](https://www.investopedia.com/articles/trading/10/position-sizing.asp) is a foundational risk management concept. It is the mathematical process of determining how many units of an asset (shares, lots, or contracts) you should purchase to keep your potential loss within a predefined limit.

Instead of buying a random number of shares or putting your entire account into one trade, position sizing forces you to work backward from your maximum acceptable loss.

## The Position Sizing Formula

To maintain consistent risk, you need a reliable way to calculate your size. The standard formula used across most markets is:

**Position Size = Dollar Risk ÷ Trade Risk Per Share**

To break that down further:
- **Dollar Risk** = Account Size × Risk Percentage
- **Trade Risk Per Share** = Entry Price − Stop-Loss Price

So the complete formula is:
**Position Size = (Account Size × Risk %) ÷ (Entry Price − Stop-Loss Price)**

*Note: For forex trading, the calculation often involves pip values and exchange rates, but the core logic of limiting risk remains the same.*

## Example Calculation

Let's walk through an example to see how the formula works in practice.

Assume the following:
- **Account Size**: $10,000
- **Risk Per Trade**: 1%
- **Entry Price**: $50.00
- **Stop-Loss Price**: $48.00

**Step 1: Calculate Dollar Risk**
$10,000 × 1% = $100 maximum risk

**Step 2: Calculate Trade Risk Per Share**
$50.00 − $48.00 = $2.00 risk per share

**Step 3: Calculate Position Size**
$100 Dollar Risk ÷ $2.00 Risk Per Share = **50 Shares**

If the trade hits your stop-loss, you will lose exactly $100 (excluding fees and slippage), which is exactly 1% of your account.

## The 1% and 2% Risk Guidelines

You will often hear about the "1% rule" or "2% rule." These are common reference points discussed in [portfolio risk management](https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/trading-investing/trading/money-management) intended to prevent catastrophic losses.

However, **these are not absolute standards**.

A 1% or 2% risk might be appropriate for a moderately experienced trader in a liquid market, but it could be too aggressive for:
- Beginners who are still learning.
- Traders testing a new [backtesting strategy](/blog/how-to-backtest-trading-strategy).
- Highly volatile markets where slippage is common.
- Smaller accounts where fixed fees represent a larger percentage of the trade.

The optimal risk percentage depends heavily on your individual risk tolerance, win rate, and the specific market conditions.

## Drawdown and Recovery Math

Why are small risk percentages so heavily emphasized? Because of the mathematical asymmetry of drawdowns. Losses and gains are not equal.

As documented by [Bogleheads' percentage gain and loss principles](https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Percentage_gain_and_loss), the deeper your account drops, the harder it is to recover:

| Account Drawdown | Gain Needed to Break Even |
|---|---|
| 10% | 11.1% |
| 20% | 25.0% |
| 30% | 42.9% |
| 40% | 66.7% |
| 50% | 100.0% |

If you experience a 50% drawdown, you must double your remaining money just to get back to where you started. Proper position sizing is designed to keep you out of the bottom half of that table.

## Why Oversized Trades Make Recovery Harder

Imagine a scenario where a trader decides to risk 10% of their account on a single trade because the setup looks highly favorable.

If the trade fails, the account suffers a 10% drawdown immediately. According to the recovery math table, they now need an 11.1% gain on their remaining balance just to break even. If they encounter a natural losing streak of three trades at 10% risk, the account is down nearly 30%, requiring a 42.9% return to recover.

By contrast, if the trader had risked 1%, a three-trade losing streak results in a roughly 3% drawdown, which is mathematically much easier to recover from. [Recovering from a trading loss](/blog/how-to-recover-from-trading-loss) is primarily a function of how deep the hole is to begin with.

## Position Sizing vs. Stop-Loss vs. Risk-Reward

It is important to understand how position sizing interacts with other risk concepts:
- **Stop-Loss**: Determines *where* you exit a losing trade to cap your loss.
- **Position Sizing**: Determines *how much* you buy so that the stop-loss equals your planned dollar risk.
- **[Risk-Reward Ratio](/blog/risk-reward-ratio-explained)**: Compares your potential profit to your potential loss.

All three work together. A wide stop-loss requires a smaller position size to maintain the same dollar risk.

## Common Beginner Mistakes

1. **Using fixed share sizes**: Buying 100 shares of every setup ignores the volatility and stop-loss distance of different assets.
2. **Sizing based on confidence**: Risking more capital because a setup "feels" like a sure thing often leads to oversized losses.
3. **Ignoring portfolio heat**: Risking 2% on five different highly correlated trades means your actual exposure might be closer to 10%.
4. **Confusing margin with risk**: The capital required to open a leveraged position is not the same as the potential loss if the trade goes against you.

## When to Reduce Risk Size

Prudent traders often scale down their risk percentage (e.g., to 0.5% or less) under certain conditions:
- When learning a new market or timeframe.
- During periods of extreme market volatility or unpredictable news events.
- When experiencing a prolonged drawdown.
- When trading illiquid assets where stop-loss orders might suffer significant slippage.

## Limitations and Risk Warning

**Position sizing does not guarantee profits.** It is a defensive strategy meant to manage drawdowns.

If a trading strategy has a negative mathematical expectancy, no position sizing formula can prevent eventual losses; it will simply change the speed at which those losses occur. Furthermore, in fast-moving markets, stop-loss orders are not guaranteed to execute at your exact price, meaning your actual losses can sometimes exceed your calculated risk.

## FAQ

### What is position sizing in trading?

Position sizing is the risk management process of determining how many shares, contracts, or lots to trade based on your total account equity and the distance to your stop-loss. It helps traders control their maximum potential loss on any single trade.

### What is the position sizing formula?

The standard position sizing formula is: Position Size = (Account Size × Risk Percentage) ÷ (Entry Price − Stop-Loss Price). This calculation tells you exactly how many units to buy so that a stopped-out trade only costs your predetermined dollar risk.

### What does 1% risk per trade mean?

Risking 1% per trade means structuring your position size so that if your stop-loss is triggered, you lose exactly 1% of your total account capital. For example, on a $10,000 account, a 1% risk means a maximum loss of $100 per trade.

### Is 2% risk per trade always safe?

No. While 1% or 2% are common guidelines, 2% risk per trade is not universally safe for everyone. It depends on your account size, trading experience, strategy win rate, and market volatility. Beginners or those trading highly volatile markets may need to risk much less.

### Why does stop-loss distance affect position size?

Stop-loss distance defines the dollar risk per share. If your stop-loss is wide, you stand to lose more money per share if the trade goes against you. Therefore, you must buy fewer shares to keep your total trade risk within your predefined percentage limit.

### How does position sizing reduce drawdown risk?

Consistent position sizing limits the impact of inevitable losing streaks. By risking a small fraction of your account per trade, you can endure multiple consecutive losses without severely depleting your capital, making it mathematically easier to recover.

### Can position sizing guarantee profits?

No. Position sizing is a defensive risk management tool designed to help preserve capital and control drawdowns. It cannot turn an unprofitable trading strategy into a profitable one, and it does not guarantee future results.

### How can I practice position sizing without risking real money?

You can practice position sizing using a chart replay tool. Set a simulated starting balance, calculate your position size using the formula before every paper trade, and track how different risk levels affect your simulated equity curve over time.
