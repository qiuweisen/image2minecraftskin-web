I keep seeing the same question in trading forums: "Can I trade futures with $500?" The answer is technically yes, but the full picture is more complicated than the margin requirements suggest.

A lot of new traders assume futures are only for funded accounts or professionals. That was mostly true ten years ago. But the introduction of micro and nano contracts changed things. You can now hold a position in the S&P 500, Nasdaq, gold, crude oil, or bitcoin with a few hundred dollars of margin.

Whether that's a good idea is a different question.

---

## The difference between margin and what you actually need

Futures margin isn't like stock margin. You're not borrowing money. It's a performance bond — a deposit your broker holds to guarantee you can cover losses. When someone says the margin on Micro E-mini S&P futures is $1,320, they mean you need $1,320 in your account to hold one contract overnight.

But here's what catches people: that's the exchange minimum. Your broker might require more. And trading with exactly the margin requirement means any adverse tick starts eating into your buffer. A $200 move against you on a $1,320 margin balance means you're getting a margin call before your coffee cools down.

The margin gets you in the door. What keeps you in the trade is the cushion above it.

A reasonable starting point: have at least 3-4x the day-trading margin in your account. If the day-trade margin is $50, keep $200 in the account. If it's $1,320, you probably want $4,000-5,000 minimum. These numbers sound less exciting than "trade with $50," but they reflect how futures actually work when the market moves against you.

## The cheapest futures contracts right now

Here's a breakdown of the lowest-margin futures contracts available on CME and other exchanges, sorted roughly by margin requirement. All margin figures are approximate and change based on volatility — check your broker for current numbers.

### Micro E-mini futures (CME)

These are the workhorses for small-account futures trading.

| Contract | Symbol | Tick size | Tick value | Day trade margin (approx.) | Overnight margin (approx.) |
|----------|--------|-----------|------------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| Micro E-mini S&P 500 | MES | 0.25 | $1.25 | $50-100 | $1,320 |
| Micro E-mini Nasdaq-100 | MNQ | 0.25 | $0.50 | $100-200 | $1,850 |
| Micro E-mini Dow | MYM | 1.00 | $0.50 | $50-100 | $880 |
| Micro E-mini Russell 2000 | M2K | 0.10 | $0.50 | $50-100 | $700 |

Day-trade margins are broker-specific. Some brokers offer as low as $40-50 for MES during market hours. Overnight margins are set by the exchange and are much higher.

The Micro E-mini S&P (MES) is the most popular. One point of S&P movement equals $5. Compare that to the full E-mini (ES), where one point is $50. Same market exposure in percentage terms, 1/10th the dollar impact.

### Micro gold and silver (COMEX)

| Contract | Symbol | Tick size | Tick value | Day trade margin (approx.) | Overnight margin (approx.) |
|----------|--------|-----------|------------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| Micro Gold | MGC | $0.10 | $1.00 | $100-200 | $1,300 |
| Micro Silver | SIL | $0.005 | $5.00 | $100-200 | $1,500 |

Micro gold (MGC) controls 10 troy ounces. At $2,400/oz, that's $24,000 in notional value. A $10 move in gold is $100 per micro contract. Manageable.

Micro silver can be trickier because silver is more volatile than gold in percentage terms. It's cheap to enter, but the price swings can be aggressive. A 2% day in silver is normal. On 10 ounces at roughly $30/oz, that's a $6 move, or $60 per contract. Not huge, but with a $200 day-trade margin, your cushion disappears fast.

### Micro crude oil and natural gas (NYMEX)

| Contract | Symbol | Tick size | Tick value | Day trade margin (approx.) | Overnight margin (approx.) |
|----------|--------|-----------|------------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| Micro WTI Crude | MCL | $0.01 | $1.00 | $100-300 | $950 |

Micro crude oil (MCL) is 100 barrels, versus the standard contract's 1,000 barrels. At $80/barrel, that's $8,000 in notional value per micro contract.

Crude oil moves fast. I mean really fast. A $1 move in crude — which can happen in 20 minutes on inventory day — is $100 per micro contract. That's fine if you have $3,000 in your account. It's painful if you have $500 and your stop just got hit.

### Micro bitcoin and ether (CME)

| Contract | Symbol | Tick size | Tick value | Day trade margin (approx.) | Overnight margin (approx.) |
|----------|--------|-----------|------------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| Micro Bitcoin | MBT | $5.00 | $0.50 | $500-1,000 | $2,200 |
| Micro Ether | MET | $0.50 | $0.25 | $200-400 | $500 |

Crypto futures have higher margins than equity index micros because of the volatility. Bitcoin can swing 5% in a day without raising anyone's eyebrows. On a micro bitcoin contract (1/10th of one bitcoin), a 5% move at $100,000 BTC is $500. That's the entire day-trade margin requirement.

Micro ether is cheaper to trade, but ETH's volatility profile is similar. These contracts are fine for experienced futures traders who want regulated crypto exposure. For someone with a $1,000 account learning to trade, I'd stay away from crypto futures.

## Contracts that look cheap but aren't

A few contracts have low margins that can mislead newer traders about the actual risk.

**Micro natural gas** — Natural gas is one of the most volatile commodity futures. A 3-5% daily move is routine. A 10% day isn't even rare. Low margin doesn't mean low risk.

**Any contract during high-volatility events** — Margin requirements increase when the exchange expects larger price swings. That $50 day-trade margin on MES? It can jump to $200+ during Fed announcements or market panics. Some brokers raise margins before major economic releases automatically.

**Seasonal commodity contracts** — Agricultural futures (corn, wheat, soybeans) can look cheap on paper but are subject to weather shocks and USDA reports that move prices 3-5% in seconds. The liquidity in micro versions is also thinner than equity index micros.

## What "cheap" really means for your trading

The cheapest contract isn't necessarily the best contract for a small account. Here's how I think about it:

**Liquidity matters more than margin.** MES trades over 2 million contracts daily. The bid-ask spread is tight (usually 1 tick = $1.25). A thinly traded micro contract might save you $50 in margin but cost you $5-10 per trade in wider spreads. Over dozens of trades, the spread cost adds up to more than the margin difference.

**Volatility relative to margin.** Calculate how much the contract typically moves per day in dollar terms, then compare that to the margin. If a contract requires $100 margin but averages $80 in daily range per contract, you're basically at the mercy of any normal day's movement. A contract with $1,000 margin but a $200 daily range per contract gives you more room to be wrong without getting stopped out.

**Commission as percentage of profit.** On a micro contract where your typical gain is $30-50, a $2.50 round-trip commission is 5-8% of your profit. On a standard contract where you're targeting $300-500, the same commission structure is under 1%. Small accounts face a structural disadvantage in commission efficiency.

## A realistic small-account futures setup

Here's what a reasonable starting position looks like for someone with $2,000-5,000:

**Primary instrument:** Micro E-mini S&P 500 (MES) or Micro E-mini Nasdaq (MNQ). Both have deep liquidity, tight spreads, and predictable behavior around support/resistance levels.

**Position size:** 1 contract. Maybe 2 after you've built some cushion and have consistent results. I know it's not exciting. One contract of MES where a 10-point move is $50. But consistency at 1 contract is the foundation that lets you eventually size up.

**Risk per trade:** No more than 1-2% of your account. With $3,000, that's $30-60. On MES, a 24-point stop loss is $30, a 48-point stop is $60. These are reasonable stop distances for intraday setups on the S&P.

**Practice first.** Before putting real money into micro futures, spend time on historical charts. Platforms like ChartMini let you replay past market data and practice entries, exits, and stop placement without the financial stress. The patterns you see on historical S&P and Nasdaq charts are the same patterns you'll trade live. Getting comfortable with those on replay builds muscle memory that transfers directly.

## Broker considerations for small accounts

Not every broker treats small accounts the same way.

**Day-trade margins vary wildly.** NinjaTrader might offer $50 margin on MES while Interactive Brokers requires $500. The difference comes from the broker's risk appetite, not the exchange rules.

**Minimum account size.** Some brokers have no minimum for futures accounts. Others require $2,000 or $5,000. Check before you start the application process.

**Commission structure.** For small accounts, per-contract commissions matter. Common range: $0.50-2.50 per side (so $1.00-5.00 round trip). On micro contracts where profits are small, the cheaper commission brokers have a real edge.

**Data fees.** CME real-time data costs $3-15/month depending on the exchange and whether you qualify as a non-professional subscriber. This is on top of whatever your broker charges for the platform.

Some popular brokers for small-account futures trading:

- **NinjaTrader** — Very low day-trade margins, competitive commissions ($0.09/micro contract on their subscription plans), free platform
- **Tradovate** — Similar to NinjaTrader with subscription pricing, browser-based platform
- **Interactive Brokers** — Higher margins but lowest commissions for active traders, best for accounts that grow past $10,000
- **AMP Futures** — Known for low margins and multiple platform options

## Scaling up from micro to standard contracts

The transition from micro to standard contracts is the jump from training wheels to the open road. One MES contract has $5 per point. One ES contract has $50 per point. Same market, 10x the consequence of every decision.

Most traders make this transition too early. They have a good week on micros and assume they're ready. The issue isn't mechanics — the platform buttons work the same way. The issue is psychological. Watching a trade go $200 against you on MES (40 points) is annoying. Watching the same move on ES means you're down $2,000. Same setup, same analysis, completely different emotional reaction.

A reasonable transition approach:

1. Trade 1 micro contract until you're consistently profitable over 30+ trades
2. Scale to 2-3 micro contracts
3. Move to 5 micro contracts (equivalent to half a standard contract)
4. Trade 10 micro contracts (equivalent to 1 standard contract in everything except commission cost)
5. Switch to 1 standard contract when the commission savings justify it

This takes time. Months, probably. That's fine. The market will still be there when you're ready.

## Common questions

**What's the absolute cheapest futures contract I can trade?**
Micro E-mini Dow (MYM) and Micro E-mini Russell 2000 (M2K) often have the lowest overnight margins, around $700-900. For day trading, some brokers offer MES and MYM at $40-50 intraday margin. But remember — cheap margin doesn't mean safe.

**Can I trade futures with $500?**
Technically, yes, with a broker that offers very low intraday margins on micro contracts. Practically, you'll have almost no room for the market to move against you. A $500 account trading MES with a $50 margin leaves $450 of cushion. A normal day in the S&P can easily move 30-40 points ($37.50-50.00 per micro contract). You might survive, but you're trading on the edge of margin calls constantly.

**Are micro futures just for beginners?**
No. Plenty of experienced traders use micros for precise position sizing. If you want exposure to 7.3 S&P contracts worth of risk, you can't do that with standard contracts (7 or 8). But 73 micro contracts give you exactly 7.3 standard equivalents. Some prop firms specifically use micro contracts for this reason.

**Do micro contracts trade the same hours as standard futures?**
Yes. Same exchange, same trading hours, same underlying market. The only differences are contract size and tick value.

**Which micro future has the best liquidity?**
Micro E-mini S&P 500 (MES) by a wide margin. Over 2 million contracts per day in 2026. Micro Nasdaq (MNQ) is second. After that, liquidity drops off significantly. For most small-account traders, MES and MNQ are the only micro contracts worth focusing on.
