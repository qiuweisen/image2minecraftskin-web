The **Finviz heatmap** is a visual market map that groups stocks by sector and industry, sizes many stock tiles by market capitalization, and colors them by the selected performance or data metric. It is useful for answering questions such as: Is today's move broad or concentrated? Which sectors are relatively strong? Which large stocks are dominating the index view?

You do **not** need Finviz Elite just to use the heatmaps. Finviz says its Heatmaps remain available for free. Elite mainly adds real-time Maps data, intraday timeframes, extended-hours features, and additional customization. A heatmap is best treated as a market-orientation and screening tool—not as a buy or sell signal.

### Key takeaways

- Finviz Heatmaps are available without an Elite subscription; Elite adds real-time and intraday capabilities.
- On the S&P 500 Map, tile size represents market capitalization, so a large tile can visually dominate even when many smaller stocks move differently.
- Color tells you the selected metric, not whether a stock is fundamentally attractive or likely to continue moving.
- Finviz now offers multiple Map universes, including the S&P 500, Dow 30, Nasdaq 100, Russell 2000, All Stocks, World, ETFs, Crypto, Futures, and Themes.
- Use the Map to find where to investigate next, then verify the stock, chart, catalyst, liquidity, and risk before making a trading decision.

## What Does the Finviz Heatmap Show?

Finviz calls the product **Maps**. The familiar red-and-green display is a treemap: a large rectangle is divided into sectors, industries, and individual securities.

The current S&P 500 Map states that stocks are categorized by sectors and industries and that **size represents market cap**. That creates an important reading rule: the picture is not a one-stock-one-vote view of the market.

If a mega-cap stock occupies a very large green rectangle, it can make a sector look visually powerful even while many smaller companies are flat or down. Conversely, a sector filled with many smaller green tiles may show broad participation even if no single company dominates the image.

Think of the Map as three layers of information:

| Visual element | What it usually tells you | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| Tile size | Relative size of the security under the selected Map, such as market cap on the S&P 500 view | Trading volume, conviction, or future return |
| Tile color | The selected performance or data metric and its magnitude | Whether the move will continue |
| Sector/industry grouping | Where related companies are moving together or diverging | Actual fund flows between sectors |

That final distinction matters. A performance heatmap can suggest **relative leadership**, but it is not a direct measurement of institutional money flows.

## How to Read a Finviz Heatmap Step by Step

### 1. Choose the market universe first

Finviz's current Map menu includes:

- S&P 500
- Dow Jones 30
- Nasdaq 100
- Russell 2000
- All Stocks
- Market Cap
- World
- ETFs
- Crypto
- Futures
- Themes

Do not compare two screenshots without checking the universe. A Nasdaq 100 Map and an S&P 500 Map can tell very different stories because the index composition and company weights differ.

For a broad US large-cap check, the S&P 500 Map is a practical starting point. If you are specifically trading growth and technology names, the Nasdaq 100 can be more relevant. For smaller companies, Russell 2000 gives a different perspective from mega-cap-heavy indexes.

### 2. Confirm what the colors represent

The default stock Map commonly displays a performance measure such as 1-day price change. Finviz also provides other Map views and metrics, so never assume every red or green tile represents the same timeframe or variable.

Read the selected metric before interpreting the picture.

A red stock on a one-day performance view only means it is down over that selected period. It does not mean the company is cheap, weak on every timeframe, or suitable for a short trade.

### 3. Separate breadth from concentration

Ask two questions:

1. **How many stocks are participating?**
2. **Are the largest tiles driving most of the visual move?**

Suppose Technology is green, but nearly all of the visual intensity comes from two mega-cap companies while most smaller tiles are mixed. That is concentrated leadership.

If most industries and most component stocks are green, the move is broader.

This simple distinction prevents a common mistake: treating an index-weighted visual impression as proof that the whole market is behaving the same way.

### 4. Compare sectors, then industries

A useful top-down sequence is:

**market → sector → industry → stock**

Start by scanning which sectors are relatively strong or weak. Then zoom into the sector and see whether the move is concentrated in one industry.

For example, a green Technology block does not automatically mean software, semiconductors, hardware, and IT services are all moving together. Industry-level divergence can be more useful than the sector headline.

If your goal is specifically to study longer-term sector leadership, keep that work separate from a single-session heatmap. The [sector rotation guide](/blog/sector-rotation-strategy) covers that broader process.

### 5. Use hover and drill-down information as context

On the current Finviz Map, hovering a ticker can show related competitors and a small historical graph, while double-clicking a ticker opens more detailed information. Zooming and panning help when a dense Map contains many small tiles.

These interactions make the Map useful as a discovery layer: you can move from a broad visual anomaly to the individual company without manually searching through a long table.

### 6. Move from visualization to verification

The heatmap should generate a question, not answer the entire trade.

After a stock or industry attracts your attention, verify:

- current liquidity and volume;
- the catalyst, if any;
- the stock's own chart structure;
- whether the move is broad or isolated;
- relevant earnings or company news;
- your entry, invalidation, and position size if you intend to trade it.

For a full screening workflow, use the [guide to finding stocks to trade](/blog/how-to-find-stocks-to-trade). For maintaining a smaller list after screening, use the [stock watchlist guide](/blog/how-to-build-stock-watchlist).

## Four Useful Heatmap Reading Patterns

A heatmap is descriptive. These patterns help organize what you see without pretending the picture predicts the next move.

### Broad market participation

Many sectors and industries move in the same direction, and the move is not limited to a few giant companies.

**Question to investigate:** Is the same breadth visible in the underlying stocks and on the timeframe you trade?

### Mega-cap concentration

A small number of very large tiles dominate the color of the Map.

**Question to investigate:** Is the index move stronger than the average constituent?

### Sector divergence

One or two sectors move differently from the rest of the market.

**Question to investigate:** Is there a sector-specific catalyst, or is the divergence simply short-term noise?

### Industry divergence inside a sector

A sector looks mixed because one industry is strong and another is weak.

**Question to investigate:** Which individual stocks are actually producing the difference, and does the behavior persist beyond one session?

None of these patterns is automatically a trade setup. They are prompts for deeper analysis.

## Can a Finviz Heatmap Identify Sector Rotation?

It can help you **observe relative sector performance**, but a heatmap alone does not establish durable sector rotation.

A one-day performance Map is especially vulnerable to event noise. For a sector-rotation hypothesis, compare longer horizons, industry participation, relative-strength behavior, macro context, and the underlying companies or ETFs.

The safest interpretation is:

> A heatmap can show where relative strength or weakness is visible now. It cannot, by itself, prove that capital has permanently rotated from one sector to another.

This boundary matters because the old version of this article treated color changes as direct evidence of money flowing between sectors. The visualization does not provide that level of causal certainty.

## Can You Use Finviz Heatmaps to Find Trades?

Yes—as a **discovery and prioritization tool**.

A practical workflow is:

1. Open the relevant Finviz Map.
2. Choose the market universe and timeframe that match your trading job.
3. Identify unusually strong, weak, broad, or concentrated areas.
4. Drill down from sector to industry to ticker.
5. Put only relevant candidates into a screener or watchlist.
6. Check the individual chart and catalyst.
7. Define the trade independently of the heatmap.

The Map helps reduce a large market into a smaller research set. It should not replace the actual setup criteria.

## Finviz Heatmap vs a Stock Screener

The two tools answer different questions.

| Tool | Best question | Typical output |
|---|---|---|
| Heatmap | "Where is unusual strength, weakness, or concentration visible?" | Visual areas to investigate |
| Screener | "Which stocks satisfy my explicit rules?" | Filtered list of tickers |
| Watchlist | "Which candidates do I want to monitor repeatedly?" | Small persistent list |
| Individual chart | "Does this ticker actually match my setup?" | Trade-specific context |

A strong workflow often uses all four in sequence rather than trying to make the heatmap behave like a trading system.

## Using Finviz Alongside ChartMini

Finviz and ChartMini serve different jobs.

Finviz can help you inspect the current market, narrow a stock universe, and identify areas worth researching. ChartMini is a historical chart-replay environment for practicing decisions without seeing future candles.

ChartMini **does not import Finviz Elite heatmaps, synchronize Finviz data, or send alerts based on Finviz sector rotation**.

A clean workflow is:

1. use Finviz to understand a current screening or visualization idea;
2. translate that idea into explicit chart conditions;
3. use [Market Replay](/market-replay) to practice recognizing and acting on those conditions in historical sessions;
4. review whether your decision rule was actually repeatable.

That keeps current-market visualization separate from historical skill practice.

## Common Finviz Heatmap Mistakes

### Treating green as a buy signal

Green only describes the selected metric. Strong recent performance can continue, stall, or reverse.

### Ignoring tile size

A huge mega-cap tile can distort your visual impression of an entire sector or index.

### Assuming a heatmap measures actual fund flows

Price performance and relative strength are not the same thing as measured ETF, institutional, or portfolio capital flows.

### Comparing different timeframes without noticing

A stock can be green intraday and weak over a month. Always check the selected horizon.

### Paying for Elite without defining the workflow need

If delayed/free Maps already answer your question, real-time data may not change the decision. Pay for Elite when the additional real-time, intraday, screening, alert, or export features solve a specific problem.

## FAQ

### Is the Finviz heatmap free?

Yes. Finviz has stated that its Heatmaps remain free. Elite adds real-time data through Maps and intraday timeframes, among other platform-wide features.

### What does the size of a box mean on the Finviz S&P 500 Map?

Finviz states that size represents market capitalization on the S&P 500 Map. Larger companies therefore occupy more visual space.

### What do red and green mean on Finviz?

They represent the selected Map metric and its direction or magnitude. On a performance view, green generally indicates positive performance and red negative performance over the selected period. Always confirm the metric and timeframe first.

### Does Finviz Elite have real-time heatmaps?

Yes. Finviz lists Maps and Groups data as real-time for Elite users and says Elite users can stream real-time data through Maps.

### Can I view Nasdaq 100 and Russell 2000 heatmaps on Finviz?

Yes. Finviz added Dow 30, Nasdaq 100, and Russell 2000 Maps, and they appear in the current Map filter menu.

### Is the Finviz heatmap a sector-rotation indicator?

It can visualize relative sector and industry performance, but it is not a dedicated fund-flow measurement and does not prove a lasting rotation by itself.

### Is Finviz Elite worth paying for?

It depends on whether you need the full Elite workflow. The free heatmap is already useful for broad market orientation. Elite is more compelling when real-time/intraday Maps, extended-hours data, advanced screening, alerts, charts, or export/API access are important to your process.

## Practical Next Step

Open the free Finviz Map and answer only three questions before doing anything else:

1. Is today's move broad or concentrated?
2. Which sector and industry are most visually different from the rest of the market?
3. Which individual ticker deserves verification on its own chart?

Then leave the heatmap and verify the candidate. That habit turns the Map into a useful research shortcut without turning color into a trading signal.
