Simply Wall St is a **visual stock-research and portfolio-analysis platform**, not a trading signal service. Its strongest feature is the Snowflake: a compact visual summary of valuation, future growth, past performance, financial health, and dividends. For investors who find raw financial statements difficult to scan, it can be a useful first-pass research tool. The main limitation is equally important: its scores and fair-value estimates are model outputs, so they should help you ask better questions rather than decide what to buy.

**As of August 13, 2026, Simply Wall St still offers a Free plan.** The official plan page lists 5 company reports per month for Free users, compared with 30 for Premium and unlimited reports for the highest tier. This review focuses on the current feature limits and research methodology rather than hard-coding paid subscription prices, which can change by region, promotion, and billing period.

### Key takeaways

- Simply Wall St is best viewed as a **visual fundamental-research and portfolio tool**, not a broker or live trading platform.
- The current Snowflake has **five assessment areas**, not six: Valuation, Future Growth, Past Performance, Financial Health, and Dividend.
- The Free plan is genuinely usable for occasional research, but the current limit is **5 company reports per month**.
- Fair value is an estimate built from valuation models and assumptions; it is not a price target you should follow mechanically.
- Use company filings and primary sources to verify important numbers before making a financial decision.

## What Is Simply Wall St?

Simply Wall St organizes company fundamentals, valuation, forecasts, ownership information, screening, and portfolio analysis into a visual interface. Its company reports are designed to turn a large collection of financial metrics into a format that can be scanned quickly before you decide where deeper research is needed.

The platform currently focuses mainly on listed equities across global markets. It also covers ETFs, although Simply Wall St notes that ETFs do not fit its stock-analysis model as completely as operating companies. Its current help documentation says mutual funds, bonds, cryptocurrencies, and other fixed-income securities are not part of the platform's core coverage.

For investors, that makes the product most relevant when the job is:

1. shortlist companies;
2. understand a company's quantitative profile quickly;
3. inspect valuation assumptions;
4. compare companies and industries;
5. track an equity portfolio;
6. identify questions that require deeper filing or qualitative research.

That role is narrower than full [fundamental analysis](/blog/how-to-analyze-a-stock-fundamental-analysis). A visual research platform can organize the evidence, but it does not replace reading the original disclosures or forming an independent investment thesis.

## What Does the Simply Wall St Snowflake Actually Measure?

Simply Wall St's current official documentation describes **five Snowflake criteria**:

| Snowflake area | What it is trying to summarize | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| Valuation | Intrinsic and relative valuation checks | That a stock must rise because it appears undervalued |
| Future Growth | Forecast growth expectations | That analyst forecasts will be achieved |
| Past Performance | Historical business performance | That past trends will continue |
| Financial Health | Balance-sheet and financial-risk checks | That the company cannot face future financial stress |
| Dividend | Dividend characteristics and sustainability checks | That a dividend is guaranteed or appropriate for every investor |

Each area contains **six individual checks**. Passing more checks makes that part of the Snowflake extend farther from the center and changes the overall visual appearance.

This is useful because it compresses a lot of data into a comparison tool. But Simply Wall St explicitly says the Snowflake is **not a buy or sell recommendation**. A low Dividend score, for example, can simply describe a company that retains capital for growth rather than a poor-quality business.

That distinction matters. A large, green Snowflake should not be interpreted as "safe to buy," and a small Snowflake should not automatically remove a company from consideration. The correct question is: **which checks passed, which failed, and do those checks matter for the investment thesis you are evaluating?**

## How Does Simply Wall St Estimate Fair Value?

The valuation section is more nuanced than the old idea that Simply Wall St applies one universal DCF formula to every company.

Its current documentation says it uses **intrinsic and relative valuation**. Depending on company characteristics and data availability, intrinsic valuation can use different models:

- **Two-stage Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)** for businesses where cash flow is expected to move from a higher-growth stage toward a more stable stage.
- **Dividend Discount Model (DDM)** when a dividend-based model is more appropriate.
- **Excess Returns Model** for financial companies such as banks and insurers.
- **AFFO-based two-stage model** for REITs.

The valuation section also considers relative valuation and analyst price-target information as separate checks.

For the two-stage DCF, Simply Wall St explains that forecast cash flows may use analyst consensus estimates where available, with extrapolation rules when estimates are missing. Those future cash flows and a terminal value are then discounted back to the present.

### Why the fair value can be wrong even when the math is correct

A valuation model can be internally consistent and still produce an unhelpful estimate because the inputs are uncertain.

Suppose two analysts agree on the DCF formula but disagree about:

- long-term revenue growth;
- operating margins;
- reinvestment needs;
- free cash flow conversion;
- the discount rate;
- terminal growth;
- dilution;
- cyclicality.

They can produce materially different fair values without either making a spreadsheet error.

That is why the useful output is not just the headline number. Inspect the assumptions underneath it and ask what would have to be true for that valuation to hold.

For a broader company-research process—business model, statements, cash flow, balance sheet, competitive context, and thesis construction—use the dedicated [fundamental analysis guide](/blog/how-to-analyze-a-stock-fundamental-analysis). This page only evaluates how Simply Wall St helps with that process.

## Where Does Simply Wall St Get Its Data?

Simply Wall St states that its financial data comes from **S&P Global Market Intelligence**. Its documentation says this includes company fundamentals, management and governance, pricing, historical financials, and consensus estimates where analyst coverage exists.

For U.S. companies, underlying source material can ultimately include filings such as 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K, DEF 14A, and ownership filings, together with exchange, company, and other public information collected by the data provider.

This does not mean every number will always match another website. Simply Wall St also uses methodology choices such as trailing-twelve-month data for some historical income-statement figures. Two platforms can therefore display different ratios because they use different periods, adjustments, estimates, or update times.

### How current is the data?

The official help center says company reports are updated daily and many underlying changes can be reflected intraday. Consensus-estimate changes may take longer to flow through. At the portfolio level, Simply Wall St separately states that portfolio returns update **once per day using end-of-day closing prices**.

So it should not be treated as a real-time execution feed. If the investment decision depends on a newly released filing, corporate action, or current market price, verify the timestamp and check the primary filing, exchange, company investor-relations page, or broker data before acting.

## What Simply Wall St Does Well

### 1. It reduces the first-pass research burden

A visual report makes it easier to notice a mismatch that deserves investigation: strong growth but demanding valuation, a weakening balance sheet, declining profitability, or a dividend that looks less secure than the headline yield suggests.

This is especially useful when comparing many companies. The Snowflake provides a common visual language, while the detailed sections let you open the calculations behind the summary.

### 2. It makes valuation assumptions easier to inspect

Simply Wall St does more than show a single P/E ratio. Its valuation section separates intrinsic valuation, relative valuation, and analyst-target checks. That helps users see that "value" is not one number produced by one method.

### 3. It has meaningful global-equity coverage

The platform supports companies from major exchanges across many countries, which can be useful for investors whose portfolios are not limited to U.S. stocks.

### 4. Portfolio and screener tools are connected to the research reports

A screen can narrow the opportunity set; the company report can then provide a structured quantitative review; portfolio tools can show how holdings fit together. This integrated workflow is more useful than treating the Snowflake as a stand-alone score.

### 5. The Free plan is enough to test the research workflow

Five company reports per month is restrictive for heavy research, but it is enough to determine whether the visual format, valuation pages, and portfolio interface are useful to you before paying.

## Where Simply Wall St Can Mislead an Inexperienced User

The biggest risks are not necessarily bad data. They come from **over-interpreting compressed data**.

### Mistake 1: Treating the Snowflake as a ranking of "best stocks"

It is not. The Snowflake summarizes five categories based on the platform's checks. A company's business model, competitive position, legal risk, capital-allocation quality, accounting choices, or upcoming event risk may require qualitative work outside those five shapes.

### Mistake 2: Treating fair value as a target price

The model answers: "What would this company be worth under these assumptions?" It does not answer: "Where will the stock trade next month?"

A valuation gap can persist, widen, or disappear because the market price changes, the fundamentals change, or the valuation assumptions change.

### Mistake 3: Forgetting that analyst estimates are estimates

Future Growth and some DCF inputs rely on consensus data where available. Consensus can be useful, but it is not a guaranteed forecast. Check what the market already expects and what would invalidate that forecast.

### Mistake 4: Using a visual score for position sizing

A stronger Snowflake is not a mathematical instruction to allocate more capital. Position size depends on your portfolio, risk tolerance, liquidity needs, concentration, time horizon, and the uncertainty of the thesis—not a single platform score.

### Mistake 5: Assuming the platform is designed for intraday execution

Simply Wall St is not where you evaluate live order-book liquidity, immediate slippage, short locate, or broker execution. Portfolio return data also uses end-of-day closing prices rather than a live brokerage mark.

## A Better Way to Use Simply Wall St

A defensible workflow is to use it as a **triage and hypothesis tool**.

### Step 1: Define the question before opening the report

Examples:

- Is leverage increasing?
- Is growth expected to slow?
- Is the dividend supported by earnings or cash flow?
- What assumptions drive the fair-value estimate?
- Is the company priced differently from comparable businesses?

Without a question, it is easy to browse attractive graphics without doing research.

### Step 2: Use the Snowflake to find the section that needs investigation

Do not stop at the shape. Open the relevant section and identify which checks passed or failed.

### Step 3: Inspect the valuation inputs

If fair value is central to your thesis, inspect the valuation method and assumptions. Record the variables that would materially change the result.

### Step 4: Verify important facts against primary sources

For U.S. companies, that usually means the latest SEC filings and company investor-relations material. Simply Wall St itself recommends validating important information against regulatory filings, exchange data, or company sources.

### Step 5: Add qualitative context

Numbers do not fully capture product quality, competition, regulation, management incentives, customer concentration, or an emerging industry shift.

### Step 6: Separate research from trade timing

A fundamental research tool can help you understand the business and valuation. It does not tell you whether today's chart provides an entry. If you want to practice historical price-action decisions after completing the research, that is where a chart replay tool can play a separate role.

## Who Is Simply Wall St Best For?

It is a reasonable fit for:

- individual investors who prefer visual research;
- beginners learning how valuation, growth, financial health, and dividends relate;
- investors screening individual equities across global markets;
- users who want a portfolio view connected to company-level fundamental reports;
- people who want a first-pass research layer before reading filings in depth.

It is less suitable as the primary tool for:

- intraday traders who need real-time execution information;
- investors who want only original filings and build their own models from raw data;
- deep qualitative research on industry structure or management strategy;
- portfolios dominated by assets the platform does not cover well or at all;
- anyone looking for an automatic buy/sell signal.

## Is Simply Wall St Worth Using?

The **Free plan is worth testing** if you want a faster visual way to inspect a small number of stocks. Five company reports per month is enough to understand the workflow without paying.

Whether a paid plan is worthwhile depends mainly on usage volume rather than an abstract rating. A paid tier becomes easier to justify when you repeatedly hit the report, screener, portfolio, or export limits and the time saved is meaningful to your research process. If you research only a few stocks per month, the Free plan may already cover the core use case.

The important test is not whether the Snowflake looks impressive. It is whether the platform helps you make your research process more explicit, verify assumptions faster, and identify what still needs primary-source investigation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Is Simply Wall St free?**

**A:** Yes. Simply Wall St currently has a Free plan. Its official plan information says Free users can view 5 company reports per month, use 1 portfolio with up to 10 holdings, and access a limited version of the stock screener. Paid plans raise those limits.

**Q: What does the Simply Wall St Snowflake mean?**

**A:** The Snowflake is a visual summary of five assessment areas: Valuation, Future Growth, Past Performance, Financial Health, and Dividend. Each area contains six checks. It is a screening and comparison summary, not a buy or sell recommendation.

**Q: How does Simply Wall St calculate fair value?**

**A:** Simply Wall St combines intrinsic and relative valuation checks. Depending on the company, its intrinsic valuation may use a two-stage discounted cash flow model, a dividend discount model, an excess returns model, or an AFFO-based model for REITs. The result is an estimate based on assumptions, not a guaranteed target price.

**Q: Where does Simply Wall St get its financial data?**

**A:** Simply Wall St states that its financial data comes from S&P Global Market Intelligence. That includes company fundamentals, pricing, management and ownership information, historical financials, and consensus estimates where available.

**Q: Is Simply Wall St good for day trading?**

**A:** It is primarily a stock research and portfolio-analysis platform rather than a live execution or intraday trading platform. Its visual fundamental reports are more directly useful for company research, screening, valuation review, and long-term portfolio monitoring than for very short-term order execution.

**Q: Can I trust the Simply Wall St fair value estimate?**

**A:** Treat fair value as a model output to investigate, not a precise prediction. Discounted cash flow and relative valuation depend on inputs such as future cash flow, analyst estimates, growth, discount rates, and comparison groups. Check the assumptions and compare them with company filings and other valuation methods before making a decision.

**Q: Does ChartMini replace Simply Wall St?**

**A:** No. Simply Wall St is built for company research, valuation, screening, and portfolio analysis. ChartMini is a lightweight historical chart replay tool for practicing price action and directional decisions. ChartMini does not calculate company fair value, ingest financial statements, or provide live portfolio research.
